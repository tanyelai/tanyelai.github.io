I am a visiting researcher at the Centre for Industrial Software, University of Southern Denmark, in the group of Serkan Ayvaz, where I work on interpretable machine learning and language models. I hold an M.Sc. in Biomedical Engineering from Istanbul Technical University, where my thesis on counterfactual explanation for ECG was supervised by Ilkay Oksuz in computer engineering, and a B.Sc. in Computer Engineering from Yıldız Technical University. I went into a biomedical programme to see the data being made: how it is collected, where it breaks, and what the instruments actually give you. The degree says biomedical; the work in it was computer science.

My curiosity started with minds, not machines: perception, learning, and what cognitive capability is actually made of. I read the philosophy of language and human perception for their own sake and have never managed to stop there, because I trained in computer science and the artificial version of the same thing is sitting right in front of us. What holds my attention is where it ends up in our lives, more than any particular technique. That has led to interpretability where a model's workings can be read at all, counterfactual explanation where they cannot, and the harder question sitting under both: how you would ever tell a good explanation from a bad one. The material has changed several times and will change again, into theory, or something I have not met yet.

All of it has also had to work in production, which is a different kind of test. At present I am the architect of the agent stack at Promake; earlier I built the breast-cancer detection stack inside what became Turkiye's first MDR-certified medical AI product suite.

Research

Topics I have worked on and still enjoy learning about.

Brain science, and the philosophy of knowing

I read brain science for its own sake, mostly perception and learning, and keep the notes in public. One finding has never stopped bothering me. People give the reasons for their choices fluently, at length, and are often wrong. If that is what introspection is worth in us, what is a model's account of itself worth? And behind that, the question I actually like: when we say a system knows something, rather than that it reliably does something, what are we claiming?

Interaction, where the bottleneck is us

Capability is rarely what limits a system built for people. The limit is the channel between them, and the channel is words. A request arrives as one sentence carrying a dozen unstated constraints, and what goes unsaid gets filled in from the training prior rather than from the person who asked. Change how a question is framed and the judgment moves with it. We are the narrow part. I would rather work there than on another increment of capability.

Counterfactual explanation

What would have had to be different for the decision to go the other way? Of all the ways to explain a decision, this is the one that made sense to me. There are many true things you can say about a model, and nothing in the model tells you which of them is the explanation. A counterfactual at least has a shape. It hands you something to check instead of something to take on trust.

Evaluating explanations, the harder half

Producing an explanation is the easy part. Judging whether it was any good has no floor under it: to score one you would need to know what the right explanation was, and if you knew that you would not have needed the explanation. So the field measures proxies. Does the saliency map land inside the box a radiologist drew? That tells you how closely the model attends to what a person attends to. It does not tell you whether the map reflects what the model did.
