Profile

I work on artificial intelligence, but what I am really curious about is how this technology will coexist with people. So I do research on trustworthy machine learning and how it holds up once it is deployed. At the moment that means the interpretability of AI systems, with the group of Serkan Ayvaz at the University of Southern Denmark. Related work spans medical imaging, biosignals and language models.

Alongside research I have worked to bring such methods into practice: a breast-cancer detection product inside what became the first MDR-CE certified radiology AI from Turkiye, and, at Promake, agentic systems that take open-ended requests from tens of thousands of people.

Research

Brain science, and the philosophy of knowing

I spend a lot of my time on brain science, mostly on perception and learning, and I keep the notes in public, partly for the next student who finds the field too dark to start. Two things I read early there keep coming back in the machine work. Perception is not reception: much of what we see is a prediction, corrected by whatever the senses can afford to send. And introspection is a poor witness, since people report the reasons for their choices fluently and are often wrong about them. Both leave me with the question I actually like, which is what we are claiming when we say something is known rather than reliably done.

Counterfactual explanation

What would have had to be different for the decision to go the other way? The question is older than machine learning. Causal reasoning, philosophy and the social sciences were working with it long before it arrived here, and of the ways to explain a decision it is the one that made sense to me. Explaining anything is a literature of its own, and it has stayed open for a reason: there are many true things you can say about a model, and nothing in the model tells you which of them counts as the explanation. A counterfactual at least has a shape. It is contrastive, it points at something that could have been otherwise, and it leaves something to check instead of something to trust.

Evaluating explanations, the harder half

Producing an explanation is the easy part. Judging whether it was any good has no floor under it: to score one you need an account of what the right explanation would have been, and if you had that you would not have needed the explanation. So the field works through proxies, each measuring something slightly beside the point. Whether a saliency map falls inside the region a radiologist marked says how closely the model attends to what a person attends to, not whether the map reflects what the model did. Ask people directly and you are measuring trust, which moves with presentation as much as with correctness. That is not a reason to skip the measurement, only to be careful about what a good result is evidence of. It is the part I find hardest and keep coming back to.

Interaction, where the bottleneck is us

Capability is rarely what limits a system built for people. The channel between them is, and it is made of words. A request carries less than it needs to: one sentence, a dozen unstated constraints, and whatever is left unsaid gets filled from the training prior rather than from this person. Phrasing carries more weight than it appears to, and instruction tuning is part of why: change how a question is framed and the judgment moves with it. Asking an agent to account for itself settles less than it seems to, since a self-report need not identify whatever actually produced the answer. We are the narrow part in all of this, and I would rather work there than on another increment of capability.
