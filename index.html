Profile

I work on artificial intelligence, and what I am most curious about is how it will live alongside people. In practice that has meant interpretable machine learning: making a model's decision legible to whoever has to act on it. The work spans medical imaging, biosignals and language models, at present with the group of Serkan Ayvaz at the University of Southern Denmark.

Alongside research I have worked to bring such methods into practice: a breast-cancer detection component within Turkey's first MDR-certified medical AI suite, and agentic systems in everyday use at Promake, where I am the founding AI engineer. I would like to continue this line of work in a PhD.

Research

Counterfactual explanation

What would have had to be different for the model to decide otherwise? Asked carefully, that question marks the part of a signal a decision actually rests on, rather than the part that merely travels alongside it. I have used it to read ECG and MRI models, and to give clinicians something they can check instead of a heatmap they have to trust.

Mechanistic interpretability, and its borrowed instruments

Language models are increasingly studied with tools built for brains: graph measures, connectivity, lesioning. Some of that transfer carries its assumptions across intact, and some of it does not. Recent work with Mark Konrad asks which claims those instruments can actually support, and what a model's report about itself does and does not identify.

Explanations that hold up in practice

An explanation is worth what it is worth to the person reading it. With radiologists I have measured whether saliency maps agree with what a clinician actually needs, worked on cutting the annotation burden that keeps such systems from being built at all, and written on how a department should choose between the tools on offer. Much of what decides whether a method helps sits on that side of the screen.
